Peer Rooms

The other half of the programme

A mentor gives a founder expertise. A peer room gives them company.
Those are different goods, and they fail for different reasons.
The premise

What makes a peer room work

A peer room works when the problems in it rhyme, when the people in it can actually all show up, and when somebody present has already solved what somebody else is stuck on.

It fails when it is nine people at nine different altitudes, when half of them cannot make the time, or when it is nine founders who all need the same thing and none of them can give it.

The grouping

Four factors, weighted in order

The weights are ordered rather than tuned to three decimal places. A room that cannot meet is worse than a room with a wide stage spread, which is worse than a room short on reciprocity, which is worse than one heavy on a single industry.

1

Can they meet?

Founders state availability as morning, afternoon, evening or weekend, and "flexible" counts as all four. The score is the fraction of the room that shares its single best window. A group with no common window never convenes, and then nothing else about it matters, which is why this one is hard-weighted above everything else.

40hard weight
2

Stage proximity

Peer value comes from facing comparable problems at the same time. An idea-stage founder and a growth-stage founder produce mentoring, not peer exchange. Rooms should span one stage, not four. Founders whose stage is unknown float freely rather than dragging a room's spread.

14per extra stage
3

Reciprocity

What each founder has demonstrably already done, against what another says they are trying to do. Measured as density rather than coverage: one person who has raised, in a room of nine, is not the same as four.

10density
4

Spread of industry and need

Fresh perspective, and it keeps direct competitors out of the same room. Pairs are good, because they share context. Three or more of anything is not. "Other" is treated as an absence of information rather than as an industry eleven founders share.

4 / 3per duplicate
5

And a tiebreak: split the siblings

Two founders who share a mentor learn more in different rooms. They already have one another's context; a second room doubles it.

8per pair
Deliberately not region. The fall sessions are virtual, a third of the county fields are blank, and grouping by geography would fight stage for no gain. Geography would only earn its place if these rooms met in person.
The mistake worth keeping

Reciprocity was built on the wrong field first

The first version read the "what will you bring to the mentorship" free text. That question is about the mentor relationship, so founders answered about their own coachability: a sponge-like willingness to learn, energy and curiosity, openness to candid feedback. Real answers to a different question.

Matching keywords across them produced a reciprocity score of 78% on a room whose evidence column was empty, which is worse than having no number at all. A confident wrong number gets trusted; a missing one gets checked.

So reciprocity was rebuilt on what a founder has already done, against what another says they are trying to do. Both are facts in the application rather than sentiments about it, and the result explains itself in a sentence: somebody who has raised outside capital, in a room with somebody trying to.

Session 17 · what we run ourselves

Hot Seat

Some sessions have no guest speaker and never will, because the format is the programming. Counting those as "still needs a speaker" overstates the work left and makes an empty-looking calendar out of a planned one.

Working session · small-group breakouts · 30 minutes

Hot Seat

"Some breakthroughs only happen when you say it out loud."
Peer formatSmall groupsFacilitator guide

One person brings a real, pressing challenge. The group gets curious rather than advisory, and the goal is to find the problem underneath the problem. Five minutes on the format, then you break into small groups: decide who takes the hot seat, they set the scene without cleaning it up, and the rest of the group digs in with questions before anyone reaches for a fix.

What you leave with

  • A problem you have been circling, finally said out loud to people who have been there
  • Questions that get under the problem, rather than advice you did not ask for
  • One concrete next step, and somebody checking in on whether you took it

Rules of engagement

Ask good questions. Do not jump to solutions.
Talk from the trenches. Lived experience beats a newsletter.
Be alternative. The weird thing that worked is often the useful answer.
Follow through. If you commit to something in the room, do it.

Who it is for

  • Any founder in the cohort. Bring something specific enough that the group can actually help: "I cannot close customers outside my direct network" gets you somewhere, "I need more leads" does not.
Session 1 · what we run ourselves

Pitch Without a Deck

Peer workshop · groups of three · 30 minutes

Pitch Without a Deck

"The best founders can explain what they do in 60 seconds, no slides, no props, just words. Can you?"
Peer formatGroups of threeCold audience

Whether you are still shaping your idea or refining the pitch you have given a hundred times, this session is built to sharpen the one thing that matters most: clarity. Clarity on the problem you are solving, on who you are building for, and on why it matters. Working in small groups of three, you pitch for 3 minutes to fellow founders who know nothing about your company, the closest thing to a cold audience you will find. Then you get direct, structured feedback from people who were actually listening.

What you leave with

  • Three minutes in front of a genuinely cold audience, and what they did and did not understand
  • Structured feedback from founders who were listening rather than waiting to talk
  • A clearer answer to the problem, the who, and the why it matters

The rules

No slides. No deck. No camera. Just your voice and your story.
Three minutes each, in groups of three.
Feedback is structured, and it comes from people who were actually listening.
Said out loud rather than quietly swapped. This slot was originally scheduled as an Ask Me Anything. Founders were told that, so the change is announced on the event page itself: a later peer discussion becomes the AMA with a special guest, covering their business, the programme, and navigating New Jersey's innovation ecosystem.
One more thing the calendar has to know. A guest speaker also proposed a talk called "Pitch without a deck". Hers is a speaker session, this is the peer workshop, and they must not both end up on the calendar under the same name.
