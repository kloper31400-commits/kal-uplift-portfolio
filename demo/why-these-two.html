Match Reasoning

Why these two

Every match email states its own reasoning, and every line is checkable against the founder's own application.
No invented affinity. No "we thought you'd get on".
The rule

Every line has to be verifiable

A founder is being handed a stranger and told this is the right one. The difference between believing that and humouring it is whether the reasons can be checked.

Every line has to be something the founder can check against their own application. No invented affinity, no "we thought you'd get on": the whole point of showing the reasoning is that it is verifiable. The comment above the reason builder, and the constraint the rest of it follows from.

The consequence is that the email cannot say anything warmer than the data supports. "Investor or fundraising advisor" is a request a mentor either answers or does not, and saying so plainly is stronger than any claim about chemistry.

Read the grammar of it. The first half of each pair is something one of them wrote down; the second half is something the other one wrote down. Nothing in the middle is ours.

The vocabulary

Seven reasons, and nothing else

The builder can produce exactly seven kinds of claim. If none of them fires, the email carries fewer reasons rather than a softer one.

// What the founder asked for on the form, against what the mentor actually
// is. "Investor or fundraising advisor" is a request a mentor either answers
// or does not, and saying so is stronger than any inference about chemistry.
const MENTOR_TYPES = [
  [/investor|fundrais/i,        /fundrais|investor|capital|venture/i, "an investor or fundraising advisor"],
  [/operator with scaling/i,    /scal|operations|growth/i,            "an operator with scaling experience"],
  [/technical or product/i,     /product|technical|engineering/i,     "a technical or product leader"],
  [/built a similar company/i,  /founder|co-founder|ceo/i,            "a founder who has built a company"],
  [/industry-specific/i,        null,                                 "an industry expert"],
];

The other six: a focus area the mentor listed on their own form, the stage the mentor chose to work with, session commitment on both sides, shared industry, close enough geographically to meet in person, and an overlapping meeting window.

The shape is the interesting part. Session commitment fires every time because the solver treats capacity as a hard constraint, so a pairing that failed it would not exist to write about. Geography fires least because the programme is virtual and it is a bonus rather than a criterion.

The part that makes it honest

The email is scored by the same function that made the match

It would be easy, and completely invisible, to write a second scorer for the email: something that reads a finished pairing and produces flattering reasons for it. The reasons would look identical and mean nothing.

The mentor form is read here and scored with the same scoreMentor the solver used, which means the email states the reasoning the match was actually made on rather than a second guess at it. Which is why the reason distribution above is also a description of how matching behaved.

And a failure that would have been silent

The roster keeps three derived tags per mentor. Their application holds the raw focus areas, the stage they prefer, when they can meet and why they are here, and "why these two" is only as good as what it can draw on. So the builder reads the mentor form live rather than trusting the tags.

The obvious way to do that is to import the app's own applications module. That module imports its neighbours without file extensions, which Next resolves and plain node does not, so importing it from a script fails at runtime. Wrapped in a try, that failure costs about half the match reasons and says nothing. The emails still send. They are just thinner, and nobody can tell by looking.

So the read is done directly against five known fields, and a missing TYPEFORM_TOKEN prints a warning that says exactly what the output will be missing rather than degrading in silence.

The sequencing

Why the match lands after onboarding, not before

The introduction does not go out when the pairing is decided. It goes out after the founder has attended onboarding and finished the Week 1 Deep Work, and the ordering is the point.

1

The pairing is computed

The solver runs across the whole waiting room, scoring every founder against every mentor with capacity. The result is held, not sent.

2

The founder clears the Week 1 gate

Onboarding attended, quiz passed 10 out of 10, Deep Work written. Until all three land, the mentor is not shipped from the milestones API at all.

3

The email is built at send time, not at match time

Which is why the Deep Work section can exist. Where the founder has written it, the email stops describing them in our words and starts quoting theirs. Where it does not exist yet, the section is simply absent, never a placeholder and never a guess.

4

Both sides get the same reasoning

The mentor's copy carries the founder's application and the same four reasons. Neither of them is reading a version written for them.

That is what the eleven Deep Work sections represent: not a feature that half-worked, but the eleven founders who had finished writing before their introduction went out. The rest pick it up automatically on the next generation.

The line the mentor reads is deliberately flat about it: "Ines attended onboarding and finished the Week 1 Deep Work before this introduction went out. Every founder in the cohort does. What follows is Ines's own writing, untouched."
