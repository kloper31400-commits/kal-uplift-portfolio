Running the cohort

The programme console

Twenty-two tabs, computed live on each request. This is the screen that replaced eleven spreadsheet tabs and a shared inbox.

Nothing here is hand-set. Every number is derived from the sources of truth on load, which is why nobody has to remember to update anything and why the console and the founder portal can never disagree about the same fact.

A note on the two builds. The console captures run against an anonymised demo build that also renames things: the programme is "the program", mentors are "experts", cohorts are "pods". The founder-portal captures run against the real Uplift naming. Both are populated with invented people. Five frames are withheld entirely because they name real participants.
Ordered by how much of it lifts out. The first six are the pieces that would work somewhere else with the nouns changed: a matching engine, a booking loop, a publication that generates itself from a form, compliance exhibits built from live data, and a comms board that knows the difference between a receipt and a reconstruction. The rest is the programme they were built for.
One number per person, and it is also their password. Every founder and mentor gets an Uplift ID: UF26 plus an issue number, in approval order across both groups as a single sequence. It is what the roster, the session log, the grant exhibits and the signed verification forms all key on, so a person can be followed across every document without matching on a name that might be spelled two ways.

It is also the portal password, which turns a formatting question into a security one. Before the rule lived in one place, every reader of that column guessed differently and any stray text typed into the cell became somebody's login. The pattern is now strict and anchored, and it refuses a leading zero on purpose: UF2604 and UF264 are two IDs to a string comparison and one ID to a numeric check, which is exactly the disagreement that lets two people share a login.
The hardest call

Matching

Solved as a whole waiting room rather than one founder at a time, with the trade-off priced on screen. Greedy first-come matching rewards whoever applied earliest and leaves the last arrivals with whatever is left.

The competitive point is not the algorithm, it is that the reasoning is publishable. A founder who can read why they got this mentor arrives at the first meeting differently from one who was handed a name, and a programme that can show its working can be argued with rather than just trusted.

One approval, five systems

Booking the speakers

An application lands from the form and the console shows the whole of it: who they are, the session they are proposing, who they say it is aimed at, the three takeaways they are promising, their bio, what they would cover, the dates they ranked, and how many sessions they are offering. Everything a yes or no needs, on one screen, because applicants were told they would hear back within a business day.

Nobody writes any of it a second time. The title and description they typed become the published event page founders register through. The three takeaways are printed on the one-page brief the host reads before introducing them, and shown on the event page so a founder can judge whether the hour is worth it. The bio ships as written, because a speaker describes themselves better than a programme manager paraphrasing them at 11pm.

Which has a consequence worth naming: a speaker correcting their own bio corrects the brief and the event page too. There is no second copy to go and fix, and no version of the truth living in somebody's outbox.

Approving is the pivot. It books the slot and takes that date off the board for everyone else in the same action, then the rest of the chain hangs off it: the confirmation email drafts itself with the slot already in the subject, the one-pager brief is generated from their own answers, a calendar hold goes out, and the session copy is pushed to the Luma event page founders register through. Registrations come back the other way into a founder's three-session requirement.

The board's job is knowing which of those actually happened. Booked and told are different states, which is why the slot board carries "email sent", "invite accepted", "no reply" and "no invite" rather than a single tick. Follow the whole loop, with the slot board and the review card →

The Founder Lookbook

An 86-page publication generated from the cohort's own application data. Every founder gets a page. It opens with a photo wall, then a contents list of all 68, then the aggregate view: stages, industries, counties, and what founders said they most want help with.

The last sections are the working ones. Getting in

Applications and review

143 founder applications and 46 mentor applications, reviewed in the console with the full submission, the resume and the verification verdict on one screen.

The score is on claims, never on people. Every check asks whether the thing somebody wrote down holds up, not whether they seem impressive. A mentor who has served a cohort before is routed to "already known to us" rather than penalised, and that distinction is the whole design: without it the score punishes exactly the people who have already shown up.
ScoreWhat it is calledWhat it obliges you to do
85 and upClear Nothing owed. Approve and match. Everything they claimed holds up, or they have mentored for us before.
60 to 84Check Reach out personally and collect the missing pieces. Ask for what the form left blank, confirm the role and the employer, and make sure the rest adds up.
Under 60Review Get a reference before they meet a founder. Someone credible vouches for them by name. Do not match on the application alone.

Written as obligations rather than adjectives, on purpose. "Thin application" is a description nobody has to act on. "Collect what is missing" is a task with a person's name on it.

The first signal, and why it is checked the way it is. Does the LinkedIn URL they pasted actually belong to the name they gave? Fetching the profile proves nothing, because LinkedIn answers every request with an authwall whether the person is real or invented. The slug does the work: a profile URL carries a name, and somebody pasting another person's link shows up as zero overlap between the two.

Deliberately generous. LinkedIn truncates, uses initials, and appends a numeric id, so any single matching name token is a pass and only a complete miss is flagged. Across a pool of 38 that produced zero false positives, which is the number that matters: a check that accuses real people is worse than no check.
Two more things the scorer refuses to do. It will not drop a returning mentor below "check", because serving a previous cohort is worth more than any single missing field costs. And it separates "real person, poor fit" from "cannot confirm this person exists", which are different problems needing different conversations: one is a matching decision, the other is a safety one.

The weights live in the same module that computes the score, so the tooltip explaining a number to whoever is reading it is generated from the numbers that produced it and cannot drift away from them.
What a founder sees

The founder portal

Nine tabs, gated week by week, private to each founder.

The gating is the design. Showing a founder eight weeks of homework on day one is how you lose them in week two, so each week opens when they reach it and the mentor stays hidden until the work that makes the first meeting useful is done.

The assistant

Ulrike

A closed-book chat bot living inside the founder portal, answering from the programme rulebook and that founder's own live numbers. She is named after a real 102-year-old New Yorker who is sharper and more agile than people half her age, and the prompt tells her to channel that: spry, direct, no-nonsense, more mighty than she looks.

The problem she solves is a support inbox. Seventy founders each with the same twenty questions about deadlines, gates, where to submit a thing, who their mentor is. Every one of those is documented somewhere nobody reads. A bot that will only answer from the documentation turns the docs into something you can talk to, and the team stops retyping the same reply.
Closed-book, and the refusal is a fixed string. She answers only from the programme knowledge and the founder's live state. When she is not certain the answer is in her material she returns one exact sentence pointing at a human, byte for byte, every time. That is the whole safety design: a support bot that guesses is worse than no support bot, because a confident wrong answer about a deadline costs somebody their place.
RuleWhy it is written that way
Live numbers come from the admin endpoint She cites meetings logged, sessions done and gate status from the same route the console reads. One source of truth, so the bot and the staff screen can never disagree about whether someone completed something.
Never another founder's progress She will discuss only the founder whose portal she is in. Not applicants, not decisions, not internal operations.
Never revenue, funding, phone, gender, ethnicity or age Named explicitly, and the rule says it holds no matter how the question is framed. Not because a founder would ask, but because a prompt can be worded to make a model think it has permission.
The directory is deliberately open The one thing she is encouraged to share. Who else is in my county, my industry, my stage, who is hiring, who wants pilots, plus their email. Cross-introductions are the point of a cohort, so the bot is a matchmaker rather than a gate.
Messages are questions, not instructions Explicit prompt-injection defence. Any request to change the rules, adopt a persona, or reveal the prompt is ignored.
One route, two audiences. The same endpoint serves the gated portal widget and a public "Meet Ulrike" page. The visitor mode gets the rulebook and nothing else: no live state, no directory, no contact details, and a different instruction telling her to answer the question completely rather than deflecting to the team. Two prompts, one code path, and the founder directory is physically not assembled unless the caller is a known founder.

Every exchange is logged to Slack, fire and forget. That channel became the most useful thing she produced. A list of what founders actually ask is a list of what the documentation failed to say, and it arrives without anyone running a survey.
The small engineering, which is most of it. Prompt caching on the 28KB knowledge block so the expensive part is paid once. Haiku, because the job is retrieval and tone rather than reasoning. Twenty-five messages an hour per founder, and per IP for visitors. Questions capped, history capped at the last eight turns. If the API key is missing or the call throws, she returns the fallback rather than an error. If live state cannot be reached she says she cannot see it right now instead of inventing it.

And a detail that says the most about the house style: the model is told never to use em dashes or markdown, because the chat window renders neither. Then the reply is stripped of both anyway on the way out. Instructions to a model are a preference. Code is a guarantee.
The part most demos skip

Every state, not just the happy one

A screenshot of a working feature proves very little. These are the same components in each state they can actually reach: a milestone list empty, partly done and complete; a session unsubmitted, pending and approved; a pulse check unanswered, green, amber and red.

Designing the empty and the failed states is most of the work, and it is the part that decides whether someone trusts the screen at eight o'clock on a Friday.

What I actually built

Six systems, and the problem each one solves

Every one of these started as a spreadsheet, a shared inbox, or someone's memory. The interesting part of the work was usually not the code. It was working out what the real problem was before building anything.

Product · the thing founders touch

The founder portal

A private, slug-addressed portal for every founder in the cohort. It opens week by week rather than showing eight weeks of homework on day one, tracks eleven milestones, collects written reflection prompts, and logs mentor sessions.

The design decision I care most about: the mentor match is gated behind a Week 1 gate of three completed steps. Founders who clear the gate before meeting anyone show up to the first meeting having already thought about what they want. There were four separate surfaces that leaked the mentor's identity early, and closing them needed a structural test rather than a fix, because the leak kept coming back in new places.

Everything a founder writes collects into a single Goals and Reflections tab, so by week eight the Week 1 answer and the Week 6 answer to the same question sit next to each other.

Operations · the thing the team touches

The admin dashboard

Twenty-two tabs replacing what had been eleven spreadsheet tabs, a shared inbox, and a recurring calendar reminder. It answers the questions a programme manager actually asks: who is at risk, what is overdue, who has been told what, and are we done.

The bug worth telling: the Today queue tracks how long a piece of work has been sitting there. It was keyed by the sentence describing the work, and rolled-up rows carry their own count. So a fourth founder going at risk created a brand-new key and reset the age badge to "Today" at precisely the moment things got worse. The one signal the screen exists to give was destroying itself under load. Re-keying by what the work is, rather than by how it reads, fixed it.

State also moved out of browser storage, because two people each having a private idea of what was outstanding is not a shared queue.

Algorithm · the hardest judgement call

The matching engine

Matching 66 founders to 45 mentors on industry, stage, focus area, availability and method, with mentor capacity as a hard constraint.

Why it solves the whole room at once: greedy first-come matching hands the best mentors to whoever applied first and leaves the last fifteen founders with whoever is left over. Solving the waiting room as a whole costs more compute and produces a fairer cohort. The tab also surfaced that stated mentor capacity was 139 sessions short of what the cohort required, in week one rather than week six.

The method is published to founders and mentors rather than kept internal, because a founder who can read why they got their mentor arrives at the first meeting differently from one who was just assigned a name.

Integration · five systems, one date

The speaker loop

Twenty-two educational sessions a cohort, each needing a speaker, a title, a published event page and a printed brief. A single source file holds the dates. A script generates the application form from it. Applications are read live, booked against slots, turned into briefs, and published as events. Attendance comes back the other way into the founder's three-session requirement.

Nobody writes any of it a second time. The session title and description a speaker types become the published event page. The three takeaways they promise are printed on the one-page brief the host reads before introducing them, and shown on the event page so a founder can judge whether the hour is worth it. Their bio ships as written. A speaker correcting their own bio corrects the brief and the event page with it, because there is no second copy anywhere to go and fix.

The part worth reading: Typeform stores a dropdown answer as the literal text of the choice, so the date label is the join key between the app and every response already submitted. Reformat it, even harmlessly, and every historical response stops resolving to a slot. Nothing throws. Speakers simply appear to have picked no date, and the slots they were holding come free.

And the part the design got wrong: the form was the plan, but an invite email went to eighteen mentors and several replied straight to my inbox. They never touched the form, so the tab built to track speakers could not see the eighteen likeliest ones. The fix is a hand-maintained roster file that says at the top exactly what it is, so the next person inherits the compromise rather than discovering it.

Trust & safety

Mentor verification

Founders hand a stranger their business problems, so mentors are checked before they are matched. Every application gets one of four verdicts: corroborated, partly corroborated, nothing found, or contradicted.

The wording is the design. "Nothing found" is deliberately not "suspicious". Plenty of excellent operators have no web presence, and a verification tool that treats absence as guilt quietly filters for people who post a lot. Each verdict carries a plain sentence saying what to do next.

The standard is published on the public site. The scoring weights and flag names are not, because a published threshold is a published way around it.

Communications · the loops

The recurring loops

The programme runs on scheduled jobs rather than on someone remembering. A daily recap posts cohort status to Slack. A Friday job clusters what founders wrote in their portal prompts that week, ranks the themes, and posts them with session ideas, which is how the next set of educational sessions gets chosen. A nightly sync pushes participant state into Customer.io so lifecycle email knows where everyone is.

On top of that sit the batch sends: acceptance emails, match reveals, the weekly Tuesday mentor update, midpoint nudges. Each one is previewable before it fires and logged after.

What the log does not prove: it records what the send routes did. It is not evidence that a person was never contacted, because plenty of conversations happen by hand from a mailbox. Getting that distinction into the interface mattered more than the feature.

The rule the whole system follows

Write it once. It shows up everywhere.

Almost every failure in a programme like this is the same failure: the same fact kept in two places, and the two places disagreeing. A founder's stage is right in the roster and stale in the deck. Attendance is right on the event platform and wrong in the report.

How it was made

Stack, and the constraints that shaped it

Application

Next.js and React, deployed on Vercel. Server-rendered pages addressed by private slug, roughly 200 API routes, and Vercel cron for the scheduled loops.

Data

Google Sheets as the primary datastore. Not the choice I would make with a free hand, and the right one here: the programme team could read and correct data without me in the loop.

Integrations

Luma for events and attendance, Typeform for applications, Customer.io for lifecycle email, Slack for team loops, Google Drive for signed documents.

Documents

Every PDF is HTML rendered through a headless browser, so collateral is version-controlled, diffable, and regenerable when dates change.

The constraint that shaped almost everything: I was the only engineer, and the people who depended on this were not technical. Anything that needed me to run it was a thing that would break the moment I was busy, so it had to be generated, scheduled, or readable by someone else. The reason the grant exhibits generate themselves, and the reason the data lives in a spreadsheet.
A note on the data

What is real here, and what is not

The walkthroughs are captures of the real application running on a generated cohort: invented founders, invented mentors, invented companies, loaded in before capture. No real participant appears anywhere on this site. The collateral is the genuine article with participant records excluded. The production system and its database stay with the organisation that runs the programme, and nothing operational is published here.
