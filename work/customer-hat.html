The Format

What is Customer Hat?

One founder pitches. The rest of the breakout group stops being founders and becomes the customer — real people deciding whether to spend real money. No advice, no critique. Just what the market would actually say.

01

Share

The founder pitches for two to three minutes. Context first — B2B or B2C? SaaS, CPG, product, or service? — then pitch it like a sales call, not an investor deck: what it is, who it's for, what it costs.

02

Respond

Your peer group answers only as customers: I would buy if… I wouldn't buy because… I still don't understand… I'd tell my friend… No advice. Only reactions.

03

Insights

The founder writes down the three comments they heard most, then reads them back. That list is the takeaway. This is basically free customer discovery.

The Agenda

How It Works.

Here's the plan: we spend the first five to seven minutes walking through the format together. After that, the time is yours — about 22 minutes with your group of three. One founder pitches, two wear the customer hat, rotate roughly every seven minutes so every founder gets a turn. The timings below are a guide, not a law: stretch or shrink each step to fit your group, and wrap whenever all three of you have your lists.

Kickoff / 5-7 min

Huddle Up

We review the format together, then you break into groups of three and pick your pitch order. From here, the room is yours — put a timer on the table to keep yourselves honest.

Step 1 / 2-3 min

The Pitch

The founder sets the context — B2B or B2C? SaaS, CPG, product, or service? — then pitches it like a booked sales call: sell an interested customer, not an investor.

Step 2 / 3-4 min

Customer Reactions

Hats on. Your peer group shares their thoughts using the four prompts. Go back and forth — answering questions and adding clarifying context for your "customer" is exactly what a real sales call sounds like.

Step 3 / 1-2 min

Capture Insights

The founder writes down the three comments they heard most and reads them back. Then rotate — next founder up, new hats on.

Customer Discovery

Talk to Your
Customer Like a Pro.

This isn't pitch practice — you already did the elevator pitch. And it's not the pitch you'd give a potential partner, collaborator, or investor: with them, the business structure and the model belong front and center. A customer doesn't buy your model — they buy your product. So talk about the product, like it's a booked sales call with an interested customer on the other end.

First: Set the Context.

Reactions are only as good as the picture in the customer's head. Give them the frame before the pitch.

B2B or B2C? — Am I buying this for myself, or for my company?
SaaS, product, CPG, or service? — A software subscription, a thing on a shelf, or people doing work?
Online or in person? — Where does this actually happen?
Who pays, and roughly what? — Put the price on the table early so people can react to it.

Then: Make Your Case.

Set the tone: an interested customer booked this call. They want to know what it does for them — not your TAM, not your cap table. Spend your time like this.

The problem — whose pain, and how bad does it hurt?
The thing — what it is, in one plain sentence.
The customer — who reaches for their wallet, and when?
The price — the number, said with a straight face.
The proof — one metric, pilot result, or customer quote if you have it.
Steal These

Founder Pro Tips.

This isn't just practice for the table — it's the same pitch you'll give a real buyer next week. Build the habits now.

Don't Pitch Small

Chasing an enterprise client? Never lead with "we're a one-man team." They're buying an outcome, not your headcount. Prove the value you deliver and keep your size out of the room.

Show the ROI

"Cuts onboarding time by 40%" beats "really easy to use." Put a number on the pain you remove or the money you make them.

Bring the Receipts

A pilot result, a retention stat, one named customer quote. One piece of proof outweighs ten adjectives.

Say the Price Like You Mean It

Buyers smell doubt. Name the number with a straight face, then let the table react to it.

Speak the Buyer's Language

Enterprise buyers hear risk, ROI, and integration. Consumers hear speed, feeling, and price. Pitch the way your buyer decides.

Land the "So What" Early

By your second sentence, the customer should know what changes for them. Don't make anyone wait for the punchline.

The Goal

Every Objection.
Every Yes.

When somebody says "now's not the right time," they're not sharing all of their thoughts — and a polite nod hides the yeses too. Customer Hat wins when both land on the table: the real objections (price, trust, timing, need) and the real hooks — "I would buy because you said this," "that feature is the one, share more about that." It all only surfaces in customer voice, never in advisor voice. Know the difference:

Hat's Off. That's an Advisor.

Opinions about the startup.

X"Have you tried a freemium model?"
X"The pitch would land better if you led with the problem."
X"There's a competitor doing something similar."
X"I think you should talk to more customers."
Hat's On. That's a Customer.

Data about the market.

+"I would buy this if it plugged into the tools I already use."
+"I wouldn't buy it because I get something close enough for free."
+"I still don't understand if this is for me or for my boss."
+"I'd tell my friend it's like a personal trainer for your finances."
+"I wouldn't pay $50 a month. I'd pay $15 without thinking."

The Four Prompts.

Every reaction starts with one of these. Finish the sentence honestly and you can't do it wrong.

"I would buy if…" — What would flip you from interested to sold?
"I wouldn't buy because…" — The objection a founder never hears from polite strangers.
"I still don't understand…" — Confusion is the most fixable problem a pitch can have.
"I'd tell my friend…" — How the idea travels when the founder isn't in the room.

How to Wear It Well.

Small moves that make your reactions ten times more useful.

Picture one real person — you, your mom, your last boss — and react as them.
React to the price. Money makes reactions honest.
Say the confusing part out loud, even if it feels dumb. It isn't.
If your reaction matches what the other customer said, say it anyway. Repetition is the signal the founder is listening for.
Use their words back: "When you said X, I thought it meant Y."
"Now's not the right time" is a hiding place. Say the objection underneath: too pricey, not painful enough, don't trust it yet.
Yeses count double. "I'd buy because…" and "the feature that grabbed me was…" tell the founder exactly what to lead with.
One reaction per turn. Short and true beats long and hedged.
