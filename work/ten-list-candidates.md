# Things Worth Stealing — working list

**Audience:** any program that takes applications, picks people, pairs or groups
them, runs sessions, and reports on outcomes. Accelerators, incubators,
fellowships, residencies, bootcamps, apprenticeships, competitions, grant programs.

**The test every item has to pass:** name the human failure, then the mechanism
that flips it. If it's plumbing or an output, it doesn't go on the list.

---

## The twelve — LOCKED, in recommended order

Each one: the name, why you need this, how we did it, how you should do it.

---

### 1. Build a chatbot.

**Why you need this.** People ask logistics questions at 11pm and a two-person team
cannot be awake for it. And the reason most programs will not ship one is the right
reason: a bot that confidently invents a deadline is worse than no bot at all. So
the work is not the chatbot. The work is the box you put it in.

**How we did it.** Ulrike is closed-book, named after a real 102-year-old New Yorker
who is sharper than people half her age. She answers only from the program rulebook
and that person's own live record. Anything she is not certain about gets one fixed
sentence pointing at a human. She will never discuss another participant's progress,
never reveal anyone's revenue or funding, and she treats a message as a question
rather than an instruction. The first reaction from our CTO was to compliment the
gating.

**How you should do it.** Think of it as a folder. Put your rulebook in it, put
that one person's own record in it, and give the bot nothing else to read. Every
answer has to come out of that folder, and anything outside it gets one sentence
pointing at a human. That is the whole trick, and it is what keeps it clean and
simple.

Then write down what it may never say before you write what it can. And give it a
name and a personality, because people forgive a character and abandon a widget.

---

### 2. Hook your collateral to the same data.

**Why you need this.** The most polished thing your program produces is the thing
that goes to investors, funders and partners. Most programs build it at the end, by
hand, from a spreadsheet, in a panic. Plan for the finished product before you
collect a single answer and it is done before you need it.

**How we did it.** The lookbook, the posters, the certificates and the one-pagers
all read the live application. The investor-ready lookbook is finished the second I
press approve. No export, no cleanup, no copy-paste, and almost every founder's page
prints exactly as they wrote it themselves.

**How you should do it.** Decide what your most polished end product is, then work
backwards to what it needs. Generate it from the roster, and never edit the asset
again. Only the source.

---

### 3. Build the application last.

**Why you need this.** The form is the only moment everyone in your program is
paying attention and willing to type. Write it first and you will spend the rest
of the cohort chasing answers you could have had on day one.

**How we did it.** We worked backwards from every artifact the form feeds: the
pairing, the mentor handout, the lookbook, the funder exhibit, the portal. That is
why we ask for a headshot and for your superpower. Neither makes sense to somebody
filling in the form. Later one is the poster, the certificate and the book, and the
other is the first line of the email that introduces them to their mentor.

**How you should do it.** List every document, email and asset your program will
ever produce. Write down what each one needs. Then write the form. Ask in week zero
for what you will need in week six, while you still have their attention.

---

### 4. Build your own algorithm.

**Why you need this.** The highest-stakes decisions in your program are the
repeated ones: who gets in, and who gets put with whom. Most programs make both by
feel, in a spreadsheet, by whoever is free that afternoon. Write the rules down and
a decision you make forty times becomes a decision you made once, carefully.

**How we did it.** Two of them.

A credibility score behind the approve button. Thirteen weighted signals from the
application, scored on whether what somebody claimed held up.

Deliberately not scored on how findable they are, and this is the part worth
copying. Rank people by web presence and you do catch the one person who made
everything up. You also flag the salon owner who has run a real business for
fifteen years and has no LinkedIn, and the person whose entire professional
footprint is in another language. Meanwhile anyone with a tidy profile sails
through. So the score asks whether the specific things they told us turned out to
be true, and someone who claimed nothing gets routed to "have we worked with them
before" instead of being punished for it.

It ends in an instruction rather than an adjective: approve them, collect what is
missing, or get a reference before they meet a participant.

And a matching solver that solves the whole cohort at once. Working down a list is
optimal for whoever you serve first and quietly spends everyone else's options, so
every suggestion re-solves the entire cohort with that one pairing locked in, and
the tool names which other participants a given click would cost. Quality decides
it and convenience only ever breaks a tie.

**How you should do it.** Pick your two most repeated judgment calls and write down
the rules you are already using without admitting it. Score claims, never presence.
Solve the whole board, never the next row. And make the tool show its reasoning, so
a human stays the decision-maker.

### 5. Swap email campaigns for personalized portals.

**Why you need this.** This is not an argument against email. Send one or two a week
if you have something worth saying, and always send the welcome. It is an argument
for making sure that when your name shows up in an inbox, the person already knows
it matters.

Every inbox is now full of AI-written outreach wearing a human voice. Urgent. Hey
there. Just checking in. Quick reminder. Hey, I noticed you. People have learned to
delete on sight and they do not sort carefully first. So every low-value email you
send spends the credibility of the next one. Twenty-two events across a cohort turns
into a drip of sign up for this, this is happening tomorrow, thanks for coming, have
you logged your meeting, this week you need to do your meeting. Each is reasonable
alone. Together they teach people that your name is safe to ignore, and the person
who most needed the reminder is the one who stopped opening them in week three.

**How we did it.** The congratulations email still goes out, and it is the one we
build with real care. After that the reminders stop being emails. This week's action
items, the sessions to register for, the meeting to log, the pulse, the deadline:
all of it sits on one page that is always current, and the person checks it off
there.

The byproducts turned out to matter as much as the organizing. It has their name on
it and only their information in it, so it reads as theirs rather than as
administration. They can see their own progress instead of being told it. Their own
written answers are readable back to them weeks later, which is the part people
mention. And because everything lives in one place, none of it feels like homework
arriving in an inbox.

**How you should do it.** Send the welcome, and make it good. Then hold every email
after it to one question: could this be a line on their page instead? Almost always
it can. What survives that test is one or two a week that genuinely earn an
interruption, and because that is all you send, those get opened.

### 6. Tooltip everything.

**Why you need this.** Your team answers the same question forty times, and the
fortieth person never asks it. They just get it wrong, quietly, and you find out in
week five.

**How we did it.** A tooltip on every tab, every field and every piece of our own
jargon. What this page is for. What a yellow pulse means. What Bardeen is and why
you were put in it. The one on a participant's ID says: this is private, this is
yours, please do not share it, and here are the three things you use it for. That
single tooltip replaced four support emails.

**How you should do it.** Write one for anything a newcomer would have to ask
about, including your own internal words, which are the ones you have stopped
noticing. Then put a switch on them so people can turn them off, because the person
who already knows should not be reading the same hint in week seven.

### 7. Green, yellow, red.

**Why you need this.** People will not tell you they are drowning. Red means crisis
and nobody picks it, so a two-option check-in reads as fine or emergency, everybody
says fine, and you find out in week seven.

**How we did it.** One tap in the portal. Green is a-okay, yellow means a check-in
would be nice, red means it is not going as planned. Yellow or red both get a human
reaching out. The window is a whole week, so being busy on Friday does not make you
a non-responder. And "what does a yellow pulse mean" is on the onboarding quiz, so
people learn the word before they need it.

**How you should do it.** Three options, never two. Make the middle one cheap to
pick, and teach what it means before anyone needs it.

---

### 8. Win of the Week.

**Why you need this.** Nobody submits a win into a void. Ask a whole cohort for
updates and you get silence, then you have no idea what your program actually did.

**How we did it.** Optional to give, public to receive. Anyone can share a win any
week, and the ones that come in get celebrated by name in the Tuesday note to the
whole cohort. The reward for volunteering is immediate and everyone sees it.

**How you should do it.** Never require it. Make the reward public and same-week.

---

### 9. Publish the asks, not the bios.

**Why you need this.** Nobody knows how to open a conversation with a stranger's
biography. Everybody knows how to answer "I need customers." A directory of bios is
a document nobody opens twice.

**How we did it.** The cohort directory indexes who is hiring, who is looking for
customers and who is raising, alongside county, industry and stage. Those same three
answers are the first thing a corporate partner asks for, so one question on the
form does two jobs.

**How you should do it.** Ask what each person needs, then publish that as the index.
Decide what is withheld before you publish, and withhold it on the server rather
than blurring it in the browser.

---

### 10. The Hot Seat.

**Why you need this.** Advice arrives fast and lands shallow. A room full of people
giving advice talks at somebody instead of with them, and the person in the middle
leaves with opinions instead of a next step. The shorter your program, the worse
that trade gets: booking an expert costs weeks of lead time and buys you a talk.

**How we did it.** One person brings a live problem, circulated in advance. Ten
minutes of questions only, no advice until the timer goes. They close by saying
their next action out loud. No speaker, no deck, no honorarium, and participants
rank it against every expert session we ran and it wins.

**How you should do it.** Program the room you already have before you go book a
room full of experts. Questions before advice is the entire rule. It scales down to
almost any length: a three-hour event, a single condensed evening, a sixty-day
sprint, a two-month cohort. The less time you have, the better it trades, because
it needs no lead time and it still sends one person home with a decision.

---

### 11. Put ten agents to work every morning.

**Why you need this.** Nobody has time to check ten things by hand every day, so
programs find out somebody churned at the closing event, when the only thing left to
do about it is feel bad.

**How we did it.** Ten jobs, each one a question somebody would otherwise have to
remember to ask. Who has not logged in for a week. Whose pulse came back yellow or
red. Who is behind for the week they are in. Which mentor has not replied inside the
72 hours they agreed to. Which mentor is carrying more than they offered to give.
What came in overnight and how it scored. Which events are under capacity with a
week to go. Which speakers have not confirmed. What is still outstanding on the
funder paperwork. And what everybody wrote this week, themed.

Three of those run today. The rest are specified and being built, which is worth
saying plainly rather than implying a finished machine.

**How you should do it.** Start with three: who has gone quiet, who is behind, who
needs a human today. Each one runs before you wake up and speaks only when something
needs a person. An agent that reports every morning that everything is fine is an
agent nobody reads by week two.

---

### 12. The cheap stuff is the program.

**Why you need this.** Small programs think legitimacy takes budget, so they wait,
or they apologize for being small. Meanwhile the parts people actually remember cost
almost nothing, and the expensive parts are the ones nobody mentions afterwards.

**How we did it.** A $60 retractable poster from the UPS Store made a room read as an
institution. Handwritten thank-you cards were the thing mentors took home and kept.
Numbered tickets. A portal with no budget behind it. Inviting partners and actually
telling people, which costs nothing at all.

**How you should do it.** Pick the three cheapest things that would make your program
feel real, and do them this month. None of this needs a bigger budget. It needs
somebody to decide it matters.

## Bench

- **Hook your collateral to the same data.** The book, posters, certificates,
  one-pagers, all generated from the application so approving someone populates
  everything. *Kennedy defended keeping this separate from #8, then said cut
  three, and my cut took it. Probably needs restoring.*

- **The 72-hour checkbox.** People agree to a program then go quiet. A required
  checkbox on the application: I will respond within 72 hours. Now chasing
  someone is holding them to a sentence they signed, not nagging a stranger.
  *Never ruled on. Cheapest thing in the program.*

- **The thank-you station.** Everyone at an event is thinking about someone who
  helped them and almost nobody acts, because the moment never has a table, a
  pen and permission. *Already a page in the published deck.*

- **Send speakers their own words back.** Speakers drift or ghost. The brief
  returns their own application answers with format, audience and length, plus a
  confirmation gate.

- **Let what they write set the curriculum.** Read the submissions, pull the
  themes, build the sessions from them.

- **Say the disclosure won't count against them.** People won't tell you what
  they need. Without that sentence in writing, the question is a trap and
  everyone types no.
